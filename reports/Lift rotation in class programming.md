# Keep tested lifts fixed, rotate everything else

Rotating a larger lift and skill pool is the norm in CrossFit and HYROX-style class programming, but the rotation happens mostly *between* cycles, not week to week inside one. None of the published services reviewed runs the same three lifts on the same weekdays all year. The dominant pattern is a 6–12 week cycle in which 2–4 slow lifts plus one Olympic-lift slot each hold a fixed weekly slot, and the lift set changes at the next cycle or sometimes halfway through. True A/B-week alternation of main lifts is documented at only one major service, Training Think Tank. HYROX-style classes go further and rotate whole day *formats* (strength, engine, race-specific) rather than pinning lifts to weekdays. Court6's Cycle 1 is therefore conventional as a single cycle: it actually carries four barbell lifts (squat, deadlift, strict press, power clean) plus one skill, which sits inside the industry range. The thing that would be unusual is repeating it unchanged as Cycle 2. The training evidence backs the industry compromise. Swapping exercises produces roughly the same strength and muscle gains as keeping them fixed. But 1RM and 3RM results depend heavily on practising the exact tested lift, and variety measurably improves adherence. For Cycle 2 (Dec 21, 2026), the best-supported design keeps three tested movement patterns trained weekly and changes the variant or loading scheme from Cycle 1. Rotation, including any A/B alternation, should go into the secondary slots: the Friday power slot, the skill day and the Thursday station-flavoured conditioning. Caveat: no survey measures how common each structure is, and almost no trial data covers adults over 40. The conclusions rest on a small sample of public programs and on young-adult studies.

## Fixed slots within a cycle, a rotating pool across cycles

The best-documented services share one shape. A Mayhem-labelled 8-week "Surge" cycle (authorship unverified) runs Day 1 back squat, Day 2 deadlift and Day 3 an Olympic lift that **alternates snatch and clean & jerk weekly**. It tests in Week 1, wave-loads through Weeks 2–6, tapers in Week 7 and **retests in Week 8**, with each piece kept under 20 minutes "to fit into busy class or training schedules" ([Mayhem Surge doc](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1O1LePVZLBi5_8f9oK2drdvTd1v0YUkTbh_MZbOpCImI/mobilebasic)). That is almost exactly Court6's Cycle 1 skeleton. A Mayhem affiliate ran a 10-week "All the Reps" cycle the same way: a 20-rep back squat once a week, max-rep bench once a week and one Olympic day alternating snatch and C&J ([JH CrossFit](https://www.jhcrossfit.com/blog/2025/3/24/strength-cycle)). CompTrain Gym keeps the pattern slots and swaps the lifts *inside* an 8-week Base Camp cycle. Block 1 used **squat, bench and deadlift**. Block 2 switched to the "alternate" lifts: **front squat, strict press and sumo deadlift** ([CompTrain 06.02–06.08 2025 lesson plan](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/64fffe623babc063ef1c13ea/6833f08ba489d0b3c488fc3e_06.02-06.08%202025%20Comptrain%20Gym%20Lesson%20Plan.pdf)). Other CompTrain cycles run 12 weeks to a heavy-single test week, or a short 4-week 10-rep cycle ([CompTrain 10.21–10.27 plans](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/64fffe623babc063ef1c13ea/670c9e13bcb72874bd8c9e8e_10.21-10.27%20%20CompTrain%20Gym%20Lesson%20Plans_compressed.pdf)).

Training Think Tank is the outlier. Its 2026 plan maps **seven named cycles of 6–8 weeks**, each with its own strength, Olympic and gymnastics emphasis ([TTT 2026 Annual Outlook](https://trainingthinktank.com/ttt-affiliate-2026-annual-outlook/)). Its 8-week Resurgence cycle explicitly alternates A and B weeks. Monday is a C&J progression both weeks. Tuesday is toes-to-bar strength (A) or weighted pull-ups (B). Wednesday is skill plus metcon (A) or bench (B). Friday is snatch complexes (A) or overhead/back squat strength (B). TTB is retested in Week 7 and the C&J and weighted pull-up in Week 8 ([TTT Resurgence](https://trainingthinktank.com/resurgence-affiliate/)). Counting from that page, one cycle touches roughly **five or six barbell lifts and four or five gymnastics items**, but each slow lift appears only about once every two weeks. That is the trade A/B weeks make: a larger pool, bought with halved exposure to each lift.

HYROX-style group classes organise the week differently. The official HYROX365 club model has four formats (Foundational, Engine, Power and Complete), and one operator puts the scheduling rule plainly: "You don't pick the focus. The programme rotates it for you" ([HYROX Training Club CZ](https://hyroxtrainingclub.cz/en/)). One multi-track gym runs five formats along a strength-to-aerobic continuum, from a barbell "Strong" class to a long "Capacity" class ([PushPress](https://www.pushpress.com/blog/5-track-hyrox-class-rotation-doubled-race-signups)). Where free plans name days at all, they label them by pattern ("Strength, squat focus" and "Strength, hinge focus"), and the content of those days shifts each phase toward sled and sandbag-lunge work ([MyTrainerApp](https://www.mytrainerapp.io/en/blog/hyrox-training-plan)). Two strength-leaning sessions a week is the most common recommendation ([HYROX Training Club CZ](https://hyroxtrainingclub.cz/en/)). The clearest "one fixed lift per day, every week" example in the hybrid world is an individual plan, Nick Bare's free 16-week program, where "each lift is trained 1x weekly" ([Lift Vault](https://liftvault.com/programs/running/nick-bare-16-week-hybrid-training-program-spreadsheet/)). It is not a group class.

Skills follow the same block logic. Published skill programs give **one skill a 4–6 week block** with a baseline, a progression and a retest. Street Parking's toes-to-bar program runs 6 weeks × 3 sessions, testing in Week 1 and retesting in Week 6 ([Street Parking T2B](https://streetparking.squarespace.com/write-ups/toes-to-bar-intro-redo)). Invictus's masters cycle ran three low-volume tracks side by side: strict pull-up to muscle-up, handstand hold to HSPU to walk, and step-up to pistol ([Invictus: Mastering the Basics](https://invictusfitness.com/blog/mastering-the-basics)). On the evidence, Court6's single skill per cycle is conservative, and two skills per cycle is a small, defensible step up.

No count or survey of how many affiliate programs use fixed versus rotating structures exists in the material reviewed. The read above is qualitative, drawn from about half a dozen public programs. Misfit, Invictus's main track, HWPO and the official HYROX365 day content sit behind paywalls.

## The science says variation is neutral for strength but costly for the retest

The exercise-variation trials point in one direction. A systematic review of eight studies found that **moderate, planned variation matches or slightly beats a fixed menu**, while "excessive, random variation" may compromise gains ([Kassiano et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/)). The largest RCT, in 70 untrained women over 10 weeks, compared leg press every session (fixed) with leg press once a week plus two different squat and curl variants on the other days (varied). It found "virtually similar" results: **1RM +24–32% fixed versus +29–30% varied** ([Kassiano 2024](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworks2022-2026/4912); [StrengthLog summary](https://www.strengthlog.com/varied-vs-constant-exercises/)). In trained men, randomly varying exercises every session by app matched fixed-exercise gains and **raised motivation to train** ([Baz-Valle et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881066/)). Every one of these trials kept the tested lift, or a close relative, in the rotation. None shows that dropping a tested lift for weeks preserves its measured gain.

That matters because barbell maxes are unusually task-specific. A group that only practised the 1RM test gained strength comparable to a high-volume training group, with no extra muscle to explain it ([Mattocks et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28463902/)). Another study saw 1RM rise in a test-only arm that did not grow ([Dankel et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27875635/)). Frequency itself matters less than people assume. When weekly volume is equated, training a lift once versus several times a week makes **no significant difference to strength** ([Grgic et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6081873); [Ralston et al. 2018](https://research-portal.uws.ac.uk/en/publications/weekly-training-frequency-effects-on-strength-gain-a-meta-analysi/)). The implication for Court6 is specific. One weekly exposure per tested lift, as in Cycle 1, is enough. An A/B design that drops a tested lift to every other week, or swaps it for a variant mid-cycle, will tend to *under-show* progress at the Week 8 retest even when underlying strength improved. Part of a 3RM is familiarity with the lift. Westside's conjugate system, the most rotation-heavy model in strength sport, still cycles back to test the same exercise about every nine weeks ([Westside Barbell](https://www.westside-barbell.com/a/blog/how-to-rotate-max-effort)).

Mainstream strength programs follow the same split. Wendler's 5/3/1 keeps four main lifts fixed across cycles and puts the variety into assistance work ([Boostcamp](https://boostcamp.app/coaches/jim-wendler/5-3-1-boring-but-big)). GZCL fixes the Tier 1 lift and puts close variants such as front squat or RDL in Tier 2 ([GZCL Substack](https://gzcl.substack.com/p/p-zero)). Starting Strength's A/B structure alternates press and bench *within* a week and still squats every session ([Barbell Logic](https://barbell-logic.com/starting-strength-novice-linear-progression/)). The "fixed primary, rotating secondary" design is the consensus of both coaching practice and the trial data.

## Masters members need variety for adherence, consistency for technique

For a 9am group of general-population and 40+ members, the motivational case for variety is real. Inactive adults randomised to a high-variety exercise program **adhered better over six weeks**, an effect mediated by how varied the training felt ([Sylvester et al. 2016](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/variety-support-and-exercise-adherence-behavior-experimental-and-/)). Felt variety does not require rotating the tested lifts. Conditioning formats, accessories and secondary slots can supply most of it. CrossFit HQ's own coaching guidance argues that a predictable class flow is a feature. It helps newcomers, reduces skipping and frees **7–10 minutes per class for skill practice** ([CrossFit: Humans Crave Structure](https://crossfit.com/pro-coach/humans-crave-structure)).

The safety argument cuts the other way. The NSCA's position statement on older adults finds that most injuries occur in inexperienced lifters and relate to **heavy loads, poor technique and inappropriate exercise selection** ([Fragala et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31343601/)). Repeated practice of a few well-coached lifts plausibly *lowers* risk for this group, while a new variant every week raises the technical load. That is an inference, and no study has tested it directly. The same statement supports 2–3 strength days a week, notes that training to failure adds nothing for older adults, and recommends power work at 40–60% of 1RM. CrossFit's masters guidance adds longer warm-ups, mobility as "non-negotiable", mechanics before intensity and roughly **twice the recovery time** between hard sessions ([CrossFit: Coaching Aging Athletes](https://www.crossfit.com/pro-coach/coaching-aging-crossfit-athletes-practical-guide)). Two practical rules follow for Court6. Offer joint-friendly variants of each pattern as individual scaling (box squat, trap-bar pull, landmine press), not as a weekly rotation. And keep the skill choices strict and static, carrying a skill from strict to kipping to volume *across* cycles, as Mike Tromello recommends ([Breaking Muscle](https://breakingmuscle.com/programming-for-crossfit-strength-endurance-and-preparedness/)).

Concurrent training is not a reason to cut lifts. A 43-study meta-analysis found **no interference with maximal strength** (SMD −0.06) or hypertrophy from adding endurance work. It did find a significant penalty for **explosive strength** (SMD −0.28), larger when both are done in the same session ([Schumann et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/)). Power is also the quality older adults lose fastest. So Friday's power clean belongs at the start of class, at modest volume, before any conditioning.

## The 60-minute budget allows one main piece per day

The time constraint shapes the options as much as the science does. CrossFit HQ cautions against routinely pairing heavy strength with a full metcon in one class and against squeezing skill work into the warm-up ([CrossFit: Strength and Met-Con](https://crossfit.com/pro-coach/strength-and-metcon)). Published skill sessions run 20–30 minutes on their own ([Street Parking T2B](https://streetparking.squarespace.com/write-ups/toes-to-bar-intro-redo)). Once a masters warm-up of 10–15 minutes and a brief are counted, a 60-minute class realistically holds **one A piece of 15–20 minutes** (a lift *or* a skill) plus 10–20 minutes of conditioning. A second skill can fit only as an 8–10 minute B piece, such as an EMOM that alternates skill and accessory work ([EliteFTS template](https://elitefts.com/education/why-strength-training-is-equally-important-as-conditioning-with-template)). Court6's Friday, which stacks power-clean technique *and* strict press, is the most crowded day in Cycle 1. Any Cycle 2 design should relieve it rather than add to it.

The quiet race-fitness layer fits in the conditioning slots without new equipment. Programs typically concentrate compromised running into one race-like day a week ([The Hybrid Engine](https://www.thehybridengine.com/)) and rotate stations so all appear every week or two ([PushPress](https://www.pushpress.com/blog/what-is-hyrox-guide-gym-owners)). Without a sled, the common stand-ins are heavy Zercher walks, slow box squats and plate pushes for the push (Court6 has ruled out plate pushes, so use Zercher walks, box squats or burpees, time-matched); cable rows, ring rows and low-pulley pulls for the pull ([MyTrainerApp](https://www.mytrainerapp.io/en/blog/hyrox-training-plan)); and hand-over-hand rope or cable pulls and sandbag drags ([repz.app](https://www.repz.app/blog/sled-pull-alternatives); [Gymshark](https://www.gymshark.com/blog/article/solo-hyrox)). Rower and bike intervals cover the SkiErg's engine demand ([BoxRox](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=238382)). Every source agrees transfer is partial. These build the pattern and the engine, not race-day skill.

## Cycle 2 options, with trade-offs

Cycle 2 opens on Monday, Dec 21, 2026. The calendar creates one practical wrinkle (an inference, not from the sources): **Dec 25 and Jan 1 both fall on Fridays**, so the Friday power-and-press slot loses its first two sessions, and Week 1 testing would land in the lowest-attendance week of the year. HWPO and Invictus both build holiday transition weeks into their calendars around this period ([HWPO Affiliate](https://www.hwpotraining.com/hwpo-affiliate); [Invictus](https://invictusfitness.com/blog/mastering-the-basics)). If Cycle 2 keeps the same tested lifts, Cycle 1's Week 8 retest can serve directly as Cycle 2's baseline, and no Week 1 test is needed. If it changes variants, schedule the baseline in Week 3 (week of Jan 4) and treat the first two weeks as a ramp-in.

| Option | Structure | Main benefit | Main cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Fixed patterns, new variants, rotating secondaries** (recommended) | Mon squat, Wed hinge, Fri press keep their weekly slots, but switch variant from Cycle 1 (e.g. front squat, trap-bar or sumo deadlift, push press), held all cycle. Tue skill, Fri power and Thu conditioning rotate. | Mirrors CompTrain's block swap. Novelty without losing weekly specificity. Clean test/retest within the cycle. | Needs a new baseline. Cycle 1 numbers are not directly comparable. |
| **B. Same three tested lifts, everything else rotates** | Keep back squat, deadlift and strict press. Change rep scheme (e.g. from 3RM work to a 5s or wave scheme). Rotate skill, power slot and accessories. | Easiest on coaches. Week 8 retest doubles as baseline, which avoids holiday testing. Builds long-term progress data. | Least novelty. Two identical cycles back to back run against the market norm. |
| **C. A/B weeks on main lifts** (TTT model) | E.g. Mon A back squat / B front squat; Wed A deadlift / B RDL; Tue A pulling skill / B handstand or core skill. | Largest pool. Highest felt variety. | Each tested lift drops to every other week, below any mainstream novice model. The retest under-reads progress unless the last 2 weeks re-expose tested lifts. More for members to track. |
| **D. Pattern days, lift picked per 4-week block** (HYROX-class model) | Mon squat/lunge, Wed hinge/carry, Fri push/pull/power. Exact lift changes at week 5. | Best fit for the hidden race goal. Station-flavoured finishers slot in naturally. | 3RM testing is muddier. Each variant gets only ~4 weeks. More planning per block. |

A hybrid of A and C fits Court6's constraints best. Keep three tested patterns weekly with a single variant for the full cycle. Apply A/B alternation only to the slots where exposure matters least. The Friday power slot can alternate power clean and push press or jerk, which also drops the strict press from Friday and relieves the most crowded day. The skill day can run a pulling skill in odd weeks and a core or handstand skill in even weeks, both low-volume and strict. If Cycle 1's toes-to-bar retest shows progress, carry T2B into a kipping or volume phase rather than dropping it. Thursday stays the race-flavoured day, with rotating station substitutes and compromised running, and a mini-simulation in the final two weeks. Whatever the option, re-expose each tested lift in its tested form, at 3–5 reps, in the two weeks before the retest.

## Conclusion

The real question for Cycle 2 is less "fixed or rotating" than "where does the rotation go?" The industry and the evidence agree that the tested lifts should get steady weekly practice, because practice is a large share of what a 3RM measures. Novelty belongs in the cheaper slots, where changing exercises costs nothing: power work, skills, accessories, conditioning formats. Seen this way, Court6's Cycle 1 already had the right skeleton. Its weakness is a crowded Friday and a single skill. Cycle 2's job is to change the variants and the secondary slots, not to tear up the structure.

Two unknowns should temper confidence. First, nobody has studied fixed-versus-rotating lift structures in 40+ class members, so the adherence and injury reasoning here is extrapolated from young adults and expert consensus. Second, attendance at a 9am class is irregular, which means many members will miss a given A or B week entirely. That argues against heavy reliance on A/B alternation for anything Court6 plans to test. Court6's own logged attendance and retest data from Cycles 1 and 2 will be better evidence for Cycle 3 than anything in the published literature.
